Set up

One session to get Rhino running on your Mac and to see the Grasshopper canvas for the first time.

1Install Rhino and meet the canvas

By the end you have Rhino 8 running, the Grasshopper window open, and one red point on screen that you made with a component.

Idea

Rhino is a 3D modeller: you draw points, curves and surfaces at real size. Grasshopper is a second window that comes with Rhino. In it you do not draw. You place components and connect them with wires, and the result appears in Rhino as a red preview. Change a number and everything downstream updates. That chain of components is called a definition.

Two files hold your work: the Rhino model (.3dm) and the Grasshopper definition (.gh). For structural work the definition is the valuable one. It is the rule that makes the geometry, so one definition can produce a hundred variants of a roof.

Example: install on your Mac

  1. Download Rhino 8 for Mac. The evaluation is the full version and runs for 90 days. After that, saving and plug-ins stop until you buy a licence.
  2. A student licence costs ¥39,600 in Japan and is the same program as the commercial one. One licence covers both Mac and Windows, so it moves with you to a Windows PC later. You need proof of student status.
  3. Open Rhino and start a new model from the template Large Objects - Meters. This course works in metres because the analysis plug-in reads coordinates as metres.
  4. Click in the command line, type Grasshopper and press Enter. The canvas opens in its own window.

In Rhino: your first component

  1. Double-click an empty spot on the canvas. A search box appears. Type Construct Point and press Enter.
  2. Look at the Rhino Perspective viewport. A small red point sits at the origin. Red means it is a Grasshopper preview, not a real Rhino object yet.
  3. Double-click the canvas again, type Number Slider, press Enter. Drag from the slider's right edge to the X input of the point.
  4. Drag the slider. The point moves along the red X axis.

Watch out: units in Japan

Japanese structural drawings are in millimetres, and this course models in metres. Neither is wrong. What matters is that you know which one a file uses before you type a number. Type Units in Rhino to check.

Practice

Predict. You drag the slider wired into the X input of Construct Point. Which way does the red point move in Perspective?

X is the red axis in every Rhino viewport, Y is green and Z is blue. The preview updates as you drag; baking is only for keeping a copy.

Predict. What does a red object in the Rhino viewport tell you?

Grasshopper draws its results in red by default. Selected Grasshopper geometry turns green. To get a real Rhino object you bake it, which you do in Session 6.

2Move around and draw in 3D

By the end you can orbit, pan and zoom without thinking, and draw a line by typing coordinates.

Idea

3Objects, units, snaps and layers

By the end you can name the five kinds of geometry Grasshopper passes around, and you know why two nodes half a millimetre apart are a problem.

Idea

Everything Grasshopper makes is one of a few Rhino object types. Each has a structural meaning.

Rhino objectWhat it isWhat it stands for in a structure
PointA position x, y, zA node (節点), a support, a load position
CurveA line, polyline, arc or free curveA member centre line (部材芯): beam, column, brace
SurfaceA smooth sheet with two directions, U and VA slab, wall or shell before meshing
BrepSurfaces joined into a solid or shellA member drawn with its real section
MeshFlat faces joined at verticesShell elements for analysis

A structural model is mostly points and lines. Two members are connected only when their end points are at the same place. Rhino decides "the same place" with the document tolerance, which you see with the Units command. With metres, a tolerance of 0.001 means 1 mm.

In Rhino

  1. Type Units. Confirm the model units are metres and read the absolute tolerance.
  2. Turn on Osnap at the bottom of the window and tick End, Mid and Int. Draw a line (Line) from the top of one column to the top of the next. The cursor should jump to the end point. That is a girder (大梁).
  3. Open the Layers panel (Layer). Make three layers named C, G and Grid, each with its own colour. Select the columns and move them to C, the girders to G, the points to Grid.
  4. Select a girder and turn on the Gumball. Drag the blue arrow to move it in Z. Undo it.

Watch out: nodes that almost touch

If you draw a beam without Osnap and its end lands 0.5 mm from the column top, it looks connected and is not. The analysis sees two separate nodes and the beam hangs in the air. Geometry made by Grasshopper avoids this because both members are built from the same point.

Practice

Predict. Your model is in metres with an absolute tolerance of 0.001. Two beam ends are 0.4 mm apart. How does Rhino treat them?

0.4 mm is less than the 1 mm tolerance, so Rhino commands treat them as touching. An analysis plug-in may use its own tolerance, though. Karamba merges nodes closer than its own limit distance, so check both.

Predict. Which Rhino object type becomes a shell element in a structural analysis?

Analysis plug-ins build shell elements from mesh faces. A surface or brep is usually meshed first.

5A parametric portal frame

By the end you have a portal frame (門形ラーメン) driven by a span slider and a height slider.

Idea

One slider can feed many inputs. That is the whole point of a parametric model: the span is stored once, and every point that depends on it follows. Here the span L goes to the X of two points and the height H goes to the Z of two points.

Build frames in the XZ plane, which is the Front view. Z is up, and the analysis plug-in applies gravity along Z.

Example

Four points: A at the left base, B at the left top, C at the right top, D at the right base. Polyline joins them in the order you wire them. Explode breaks the polyline back into three separate members, because an analysis needs one line per member. Length then returns three numbers, one per segment.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. Place four Construct Point components and two sliders. Wire L and H as in the figure. Leave Y empty on all four.
  2. Place Polyline. Wire A into V, then hold Shift and wire B, C and D into the same input, in that order.
  3. Add Explode and Length, and read the lengths in a panel.
  4. Select everything, press Cmd + G to group it, and type a name on the group: Portal frame geometry.

Watch out: wiring order is list order

When several wires enter one input, the data arrives in the order you connected them. Wire D before C and the polyline crosses itself. If a shape looks wrong, plug the input into a panel and check the order.

Practice

6Rhino geometry in, baked geometry out

By the end you can bring a curve you drew in Rhino into a definition, and send Grasshopper's result back to Rhino on a layer.

Idea

Data crosses between the two windows in both directions. Going in, a Curve or Point parameter can reference an object in the Rhino model: move the object and the definition updates. Going out, Bake turns the red preview into real Rhino objects. Baked objects are a copy. They do not update when you move a slider afterwards.

At an office the usual pattern is: the architect's roof line or surface comes in by reference, your definition generates the structure on it, and you bake the result when a version is agreed.

Example

In Rhino

  1. In the Front viewport, draw a gentle roof line about 24 m long with Curve.
  2. In Grasshopper place a Curve parameter (Params, Geometry). Right-click it, choose Set one Curve, and click your roof line.
  3. Wire it into Divide Curve (Curve, Division) with a slider on N. Red points appear along the line.
  4. In Rhino, drag a control point of the roof line (turn them on with PointsOn). The points follow.
  5. Right-click Divide Curve, choose Bake, pick a layer, and confirm. The points are now real Rhino points.
  6. Right-click the Curve parameter and choose Internalise data. The curve now lives inside the .gh file and no longer needs the Rhino object.

Keep definitions readable from the first day: inputs on the left, logic in the middle, results on the right, every stage in a named group. Double-click a wire to add a Relay when a long wire needs a bend. A colleague should be able to follow it without you.

Practice

Predict. You bake the divided points, then move the count slider. What happens to the baked points?

Baked geometry is a copy. It no longer listens to the definition. Bake again when you want the new version.

Predict. You internalise the referenced roof curve, then delete the curve in Rhino. What happens to the definition?

Internalise data copies the geometry into the parameter. The link to the Rhino object is gone, so deleting it changes nothing.

A structure is repetition: columns on a grid, panels in a truss, floors in a frame. In Grasshopper repetition means lists. These three sessions are where the tool starts saving you time.

7Making and reading lists

By the end you can generate a row of evenly spaced points and pick any one of them by its index.

Idea

A list is an ordered set of items, numbered from 0. Most components do not care whether they receive one item or fifty. Give Construct Point a list of four X values and it makes four points. You never write a loop.

Series is the list maker you will use most: a start value S, a step N and a count C. For column lines at 6 m centres, that is start 0, step 6, count 4, which gives 0, 6, 12, 18.

8Data matching

By the end you can predict what a component does when its inputs are lists of different lengths. This one rule explains most surprises in Grasshopper.

Idea

Suppose X receives 4 values and Y receives 3. Which X goes with which Y? Grasshopper has three answers.

RuleWhat happens4 and 3 items give
Longest List (the default)Pair item by item. When the short list runs out, reuse its last item4 results
Shortest ListPair item by item. Stop when the short list runs out3 results
Cross ReferencePair every item with every item12 results

Longest List is built into every component. For the other two you place a Shortest List or Cross Reference component (both under Sets, List) in front of the inputs.

Example: the column grid

You want a column at every crossing of 4 grid lines in X and 3 in Y (通り芯). Wire the two Series straight into Construct Point and you get 4 points on a diagonal, because of Longest List. Put Cross Reference in between and you get the 12 you wanted.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. Make two Series: step 6 with count 4, and step 8 with count 3.
  2. Wire them straight into X and Y of Construct Point. Count the points.
  3. Insert Cross Reference between them. Count again.
  4. Add a third Series for Z with step 3.5 and count 2, then zoom in on Cross Reference until a small plus sign appears, and click it to add a third input. You now have the node grid of a one-storey frame.

Watch out: the silent repeat

Longest List never warns you. The component stays grey and the result looks plausible. Whenever a result has the wrong number of items, compare the lengths of the lists going in.

Practice

Predict. X receives 5 values and Y receives 2. With the default matching, how many points does Construct Point make?

Longest List pairs item by item and repeats the last Y for the remaining X values, so you get 5 points.

Predict. The same 5 and 2 values pass through Cross Reference first. How many points now?

Cross Reference pairs every X with every Y: 5 × 2 = 10.

Fix the bug. The grid should have 12 column points, 4 by 3, but List Length says 4. Click the component whose inputs need a different matching rule.

What does a component do with two lists of different lengths when you add nothing in front of it?

Construct Point uses Longest List and pairs the values item by item. Put Cross Reference in front of its X and Y inputs to get every combination.

9A truss from two lists

By the end you have a parallel-chord truss (平行弦トラス) built from lists, and you know the trick that connects node i to node i + 1.

Idea

A truss is two rows of nodes and four sets of lines between them: bottom chord (下弦材), top chord (上弦材), verticals (束材) and diagonals (斜材). Each set is one Line component fed with two lists.

  • Verticals: bottom node i to top node i. Same index, so wire the two lists straight in.
  • Chords: node i to node i + 1 in the same row.
  • Diagonals: bottom node i to top node i + 1.

"i + 1" is what Shift List does. It slides a list along by S places, so item 1 moves to position 0, item 2 to position 1, and so on. Pair the original list with the shifted one and each node meets its neighbour.

Try it here

Example: the full truss

  1. Bottom nodes: Series (start 0, step L / n, count n + 1) into the X of Construct Point.
  2. Top nodes: Move the bottom nodes by a Unit Z vector with the depth H as its factor.
  3. Verticals: Line with A = bottom, B = top.
  4. Bottom chord: Line with A = bottom, B = bottom through Shift List (S = 1, Wrap = False), with Shortest List in front.
  5. Top chord: the same with the top nodes.
  6. Diagonals: Line with A = bottom, B = top through Shift List, again with Shortest List.

That gives diagonals that all lean the same way. For a Pratt truss they must run down towards midspan in both halves, mirrored at the centre. Split the node lists at midspan with Split List, build each half, and shift the bottom list in one half and the top list in the other.

Watch out: Wrap is on by default

Shift List has a Wrap input that is True unless you change it. With Wrap on, the item pushed off the front returns at the back, and your last node gets connected to the first. Set it to False with a Boolean Toggle.

Data trees

A list handles one row of things. A building has rows of rows: nodes per floor, beams per bay per floor. Grasshopper stores those as a tree. Trees are the hardest idea in the course and the one that separates people who can use Grasshopper at work from people who cannot.

10What a tree is

By the end you can read a tree's structure in a Param Viewer and say how many branches it has and how long each one is.

Idea

A data tree (データツリー) is a set of lists. Each list is a branch, and each branch has an address called a path, written in curly brackets: {0}, {1}, {0;2}. A plain list is a tree with one branch, {0}.

Trees appear on their own. Give Divide Curve three curves and ask for 4 segments each. It returns 15 points, and it keeps them in three branches of 5 so you still know which point belongs to which curve.

Example

{0;0}   N = 5     points on curve 0
  0  point
  1  point
  2  point
  3  point
  4  point
{0;1}   N = 5     points on curve 1
{0;2}   N = 5     points on curve 2

Read a path from the right. The last number is the newest level: here it is the index of the curve. The 0 in front is left over from the input list. Simplify Tree removes the part all paths share, which turns these into {0}, {1}, {2}. The live examples on this page show simplified paths.

The rule that makes trees useful: a component runs once per branch. Polyline given this tree draws three polylines, one through each branch.

  3. Read the paths. Then right-click the P output, choose Simplify, and read them again.
  4. Wire P into Polyline. Count the polylines.

16Domains, Remap and Graph Mapper

By the end you can turn any list of numbers into the range you need, and shape how a value changes from bottom to top with a curve.

Idea

A domain is a range of numbers, written 0 To 24 in Grasshopper. Bounds finds the domain of a list: the smallest and the largest value. Remap Numbers moves values from one domain to another and keeps their proportions. A value 30% of the way through the source domain lands 30% of the way through the target.

Graph Mapper bends numbers between 0 and 1 along a curve that you choose and can edit by dragging its grips. Put the three together and you get the most common pattern in design work:

  1. Take an index, such as the floor number: 0, 1, 2 and up.
  2. Remap it to 0 To 1 with Bounds as the source.
  3. Shape it with Graph Mapper.
  4. Remap it again to the real design range, such as a plate scale from 0.7 to 1.2.

The same pattern sets a beam's depth along its span, the camber of a truss (キャンバー), or the size of members up a tower.

19Panels on a curved roof

By the end you can measure how far each panel of a curved roof is from flat, and choose a geometry that gives flat panels.

Idea

Glass and metal panels are cheapest when they are flat. On most curved surfaces, the four corners of a panel do not lie in one plane. The panel is warped, and the amount is called its deviation from flat. Plane Fit (Vector, Plane) finds the plane closest to a set of points and reports how far they are from it.

Three ways out, each with a cost:

  • Triangles are always flat, but you get twice the panels and more joints.
  • Smaller panels warp less, but there are more of them.
  • A translation surface, made by sliding one curve along another, gives flat four-sided panels on a doubly curved roof. Engineers such as Jörg Schlaich and Hans Schober used this for glass grid shells.

24Column buckling

By the end you can work out a column's allowable compressive stress fc from its slenderness, and find the lightest section that carries a load.

Idea

A slender member in compression fails by buckling (座屈) sideways long before the steel yields. How slender it is depends on two things:

  • The buckling length lk (座屈長さ) = K × L, where K depends on how the ends are held. In theory K is 0.5 with both ends fixed, 0.7 with one fixed and one pinned, 1.0 with both pinned, and 2.0 for a cantilever.
  • The radius of gyration i (断面二次半径) of the section. An H-section has a large ix and a small iy, so unless it is braced it buckles about its weak axis.

The slenderness ratio (細長比) is λ = lk / i. Japanese practice for steel with F = 235 N/mm² then gives the long-term allowable compressive stress fc:

Critical slenderness (限界細長比)Λ = √(π²E / 0.6F) ≈ 120
When λ ≤ Λfc = {1 − 0.4(λ/Λ)²} F / ν, ν = 3/2 + 2/3 (λ/Λ)²
When λ > Λfc = 0.277 F / (λ/Λ)²
Short-term1.5 times the long-term value
Slenderness limitλ ≤ 200 for columns and λ ≤ 250 for other compression members (Building Standard Law Enforcement Order, Article 65)

Example

An H-250×250×9×14 column, 4 m tall, pinned at both ends, carries N = 600 kN long-term. iy = 6.32 cm and A = 91.43 cm².

  1. λ = 400 / 6.32 = 63.3, below Λ.
  2. λ/Λ = 0.528, so ν = 1.5 + 0.667 × 0.279 = 1.686.
  3. fc = (1 − 0.4 × 0.279) × 235 / 1.686 = 123.8 N/mm².
  4. σc = 600,000 / 9,143 = 65.6 N/mm², so the 検定比 is 65.6 / 123.8 = 0.53.

In Rhino

  2. Buckling length: Multiplication of L and K. Slenderness: Division by iy (and by ix), then Maximum (Maths, Util) of the two.
  3. Two Expressions, one for each fc formula, with inputs λ, Λ and F.
  4. Larger Than compares λ with Λ. Pick'n'Choose (Sets, List) uses that result to pick the right formula: 0 picks the first stream, 1 the second.
  5. σc = N / A, then the ratio σc / fc. Show it in a panel, and colour the column with Custom Preview when it is over 1.

27Galapagos: the lightest roof truss

By the end you can set up Galapagos with a genome, a fitness and a penalty, and judge whether its answer is any good.

Idea

Galapagos (Params, Util) is an evolutionary solver. You give it two things:

  • A genome: the sliders it is allowed to move. Here they are the depth, the number of panels, and a 0, 1, 2 slider for the truss type.
  • A fitness: one number to make as small or as large as possible. Here it is the steel weight of one truss, in kilograms.

It tries a population of random designs, keeps the fittest, breeds new designs by mixing and mutating their sliders, and repeats for many generations. It cannot read your checks, so you build them into the fitness: if a design breaks the deflection limit, add a large penalty to its weight so it can never win.

Galapagos does not promise the best possible answer. When the search space is small, as here with 435 combinations, you can check its answer by trying them all (brute force).

Example

  1. The truss from Session 9 with sliders for depth H (1.2 to 4.0 m), panels n (4 to 12, step 2) and type (0 to 2).
  2. Sizing: for each member group, the lightest steel pipe (鋼管, STK400) from a list that passes its check. Tension: σ ≤ 156 N/mm². Compression: σ ≤ fc from Session 24, with the member length as buckling length. Every member: λ ≤ 250.
  3. Fitness: total weight, plus 10 × weight × the amount by which the deflection exceeds span / 300.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. Place Galapagos. Drag from its Genome input to each slider you want it to move. Galapagos only accepts sliders and gene pools as genes.
  2. Wire one number into Fitness: your weight plus penalty.
  3. Double-click Galapagos to open its editor. On the Options tab, set Fitness to Minimize.
  4. On the Solvers tab, press Start Solver. When it stops, pick the best design in the list and press Reinstate to set the sliders to it.
  5. Sizing inside Grasshopper is easiest if the section of each group is also a gene: a slider that picks a row from your section table. Add a penalty when any 検定比 is over 1. With a full Karamba licence, Optimize Cross Section can do the sizing instead, but it checks to Eurocode, not the Japanese rules.

Eight of the designs here weigh within 3% of the best one. When the optimum is flat, the lightest design is rarely the right one: choose among the near-best by depth, number of joints, or appearance. Say that in your report instead of quoting one magic number.

28Trade-offs and the Pareto front

By the end you can show a client the trade-off between two goals and explain which designs are worth discussing.

Idea

Real decisions have more than one goal. A deeper truss is lighter, but it costs ceiling height and cladding. A stiffer truss is heavier. A design is Pareto optimal when no other design is better on both goals at once. Those designs form the Pareto front; every design off the front can be beaten on both goals.

The engineer's job is to show the front and say where the good choices are. The client or architect then picks a point on it. Galapagos searches for one number only. Plug-ins such as Wallacei and Octopus search for several at once, but they run on Windows, so try them on a lab or office PC.

Try it here

  2. Plot the results in Rhino: Construct Point with x = weight / 1000 and y = depth. Scale the axes so the cloud is readable.
  3. Mark the front: sort by weight, then keep each point whose depth is lower than every lighter point's. A short GhPython script does this neatly; by eye on the plot is fine to start.

Practice

Predict. Design A weighs 1,200 kg with a depth of 2.4 m, B weighs 1,350 kg at 2.0 m, and C weighs 1,400 kg at 2.4 m. Which design is not on the Pareto front?

A is lighter than C at the same depth, so C is beaten on one goal and equal on the other. A and B each win on one goal.

Predict. The architect caps the depth at 2.0 m. What happens to the lightest acceptable design?

A cap removes options; it never adds better ones. On this page, a 2.0 m cap costs about 25% more steel than the unconstrained best.

29From a hanging chain to an arch

By the end you can form-find an arch with Kangaroo and show, with Karamba, why it carries its load almost without bending.

Idea

Robert Hooke published the idea as a Latin anagram in 1676: as hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch. A chain can only pull, so the shape it hangs in is the line of its forces. Flip it, and every link pushes instead of pulls, still along the line. Gaudí designed the church at Colònia Güell this way, with weighted strings.

  • Under its own weight, spread evenly along its length, a chain hangs as a catenary.
  • Under a load spread evenly along the horizontal, like a deck hung from it, it hangs as a parabola. That is why the parabolic arch in Session 13 suits a bridge.

Kangaroo works with goals. Length (Line) holds a link at a length, Anchor holds a point still, Load pushes a point. The Solver moves all the points until every goal is balanced as well as it can be.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. A 20 m line, divided into links: Divide Curve, then Polyline and Explode, gives the link lines.
  2. On the Kangaroo2 tab, Length (Line) with the links and a length of 1.12 × the link length, so the chain is 12% longer than the span.
  3. End Points of the original line into two Anchor goals.
  4. Load on every point with the vector (0, 0, −1).
  5. Entwine or Merge all the goals into the Solver's GoalObjects input. Its output points form the hanging chain.
  6. Flip: Scale NU (Transform, Affine) with a Z factor of −1. Then Line to Beam in Karamba, pinned supports at both ends, and the same loads pointing down.

Watch out: one load case only

The found shape is free of bending only for the load that found it. A live load on half the span, or wind, bends it. A real arch still needs bending stiffness; form-finding gives you the best starting shape, not a finished design.

Practice

Predict. A chain carries a deck load spread evenly along the horizontal. What shape does it hang in?

Load per horizontal metre gives a parabola. Load per metre along the chain, its own weight, gives a catenary. For shallow chains the two are very close.

Predict. You make the chain longer, so it sags more. What happens to the horizontal force H at the supports?

For a parabola H = wL² / 8f. A larger sag f means a smaller H, and the same holds for the arch's thrust when it is flipped.

Predict. The found arch carries a live load on the left half only. What happens?

The shape matches one load case. Any other load needs bending to be carried, which is why arches still have bending stiffness.

30From a hanging net to a gridshell

Idea
