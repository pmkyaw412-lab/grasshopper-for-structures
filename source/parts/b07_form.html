Design with data

Architects use Grasshopper to shape buildings with numbers: floors that grow and shrink, openings that open towards a view, towers that twist. A structural engineer at an office like Arup sits next to that work every day. These four sessions teach the same tools, and each one ends with the question an engineer would ask.

{{sn}}Domains, Remap and Graph Mapper

By the end you can turn any list of numbers into the range you need, and shape how a value changes from bottom to top with a curve.

Idea

A domain is a range of numbers, written 0 To 24 in Grasshopper. Bounds finds the domain of a list: the smallest and the largest value. Remap Numbers moves values from one domain to another and keeps their proportions. A value 30% of the way through the source domain lands 30% of the way through the target.

Graph Mapper bends numbers between 0 and 1 along a curve that you choose and can edit by dragging its grips. Put the three together and you get the most common pattern in design work:

  1. Take an index, such as the floor number: 0, 1, 2 and up.
  2. Remap it to 0 To 1 with Bounds as the source.
  3. Shape it with Graph Mapper.
  4. Remap it again to the real design range, such as a plate scale from 0.7 to 1.2.

The same pattern sets a beam's depth along its span, the camber of a truss (キャンバー), or the size of members up a tower.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. Series with step 1 and count 20 for the floor numbers. Multiplication by 4 gives the floor heights.
  2. Bounds (Maths, Domain) on the floor numbers, into the S input of Remap Numbers (Maths, Domain). Leave T at its default, 0 To 1.
  3. Graph Mapper (Params, Input). Right-click it and pick a graph type. Wire the remapped values in.
  4. Construct Domain (Maths, Domain) from two sliders, 0.7 and 1.2, into the T input of a second Remap Numbers.
  5. Floor plates: XY Plane (Vector, Plane) with origins at (0, 0, height), then Rectangle (Curve, Primitive) on each plane with its X and Y inputs set to the domain -15 To 15, so each 30 m square is centred on its plane.
  6. Scale (Transform, Affine) each rectangle about its plane origin by the factor. Then Loft (Surface, Freeform) through all of them for the skin.

Watch out: Graph Mapper expects 0 to 1

A Graph Mapper's graph is drawn for inputs from 0 to 1 unless you change its domain. Feed it floor numbers 0 to 19 directly and almost everything falls off the graph. Always remap first.

    {{sn}}An attractor point on a facade

    By the end you can drive a facade pattern from a point, and cut the number of different panels without losing the pattern.

    Idea

    An attractor is a point that changes things according to their distance from it. Measure the distance from every panel centre to the point, remap those distances, and use the result as the opening size. Openings grow towards a view or shrink towards a neighbour.

    The design question that follows always reaches the engineer and the contractor: if every panel is different, every panel is a separate drawing and a separate mould. Rationalising means rounding the sizes into a few types (パネルの種類). Five types can keep the look of a smooth pattern at a fraction of the cost.

    Example

    1. Panels: a facade surface into Divide Domain² (Maths, Domain) and Isotrim (Surface, Util). Each panel is a small surface.
    2. Centres: Area (Surface, Analysis) returns the centroid of each panel.
    3. Distance (Vector, Point) from each centroid to the attractor.
    4. Bounds and Remap Numbers turn the distances into opening factors, for example from 0.85 near the point to 0.15 far away.
    5. Scale each panel about its centroid by its factor. That scaled panel is the opening.

    Try it here

    In Rhino

    1. Draw a 30 m by 12 m rectangle in the Front view and make a surface from it with PlanarSrf. Reference it in a Surface parameter.
    2. Build the five steps above. Reference a Rhino point as the attractor, so you can drag it with the Gumball.
    3. To rationalise, insert four components between the two Remaps: remap to 0 To 1, multiply by (types − 1), Round (Maths, Util), divide by (types − 1), then remap to your real range.
    4. Count the different sizes: Create Set (Sets, Sets) keeps one copy of each value; List Length counts them.

    Practice

    An opening factor of 0.5 scales a 2 m by 3 m panel about its centre. What is the opening area? 3.0 m²1.5 m²0.75 m² Scale shrinks both directions: 1 m by 1.5 m = 1.5 m², a quarter of the panel. Opening area goes with the factor squared. You rationalise into 3 types between 0.2 and 0.8. Which factors can a panel have? 0.2, 0.5 and 0.80.2, 0.4 and 0.6Any value between 0.2 and 0.8 Three evenly spaced steps include both ends: 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8. The openings come out many times larger than the panels. Click the component that receives the wrong numbers. Read the panel. What unit are those numbers in? Scale's F input gets raw distances in metres, so a panel 12 m from the point is scaled 12 times. Remap the distances to the factor range first.

    Add a second attractor point. Each opening should respond to whichever point is nearer.
  • Openings grow near either point.
  • Moving one point changes only the panels nearer to it.
    •  Measure the distance to each point, then keep the smaller of the two for every panel.
    1. Two Distance components, one per attractor.
    2. Minimum (Maths, Util) of the two distance lists, item by item.
    3. The rest is unchanged: Bounds, Remap, Scale. A Point parameter can also hold several points; then use Closest Point (Vector, Point), whose D output is the distance to the nearest one.

    {{sn}}A twisting tower and its diagrid

    By the end you can build a twisting, tapering tower, wrap it in a diagrid, and say what the twist does to its columns.

    Idea

    A tower is a list of floor plates. Give each floor an angle and a scale and the whole form follows. Graph Mapper decides how the twist is shared: evenly, mostly at the top, or slowly at both ends.

    For the engineer, a twist means that a column following the corners leans, and a leaning column pushes sideways at every floor. A diagrid (ダイアグリッド) avoids that: diagonal members in a triangulated net carry both gravity and wind or earthquake load. Towers such as 30 St Mary Axe in London, engineered by Arup, use one.

    Try it here

    In Rhino

    1. Floor plates as in Session {{n:f1}}: planes at each floor height and a 30 m square on each.
    2. Angles: remap the floor numbers to 0 To 1, Graph Mapper, then multiply by the total twist in degrees.
    3. Rotate (Transform, Euclidean) each plate about its own plane. Its A input expects radians: convert with Radians (Maths, Trigonometry), or right-click A and choose Degrees.
    4. Taper: Scale each plate about its centre with a factor that runs from 1 at the base to your top scale.
    5. Diagrid: Divide Curve on every second plate gives the nodes, one branch per level. Connect node j on one level to nodes j and j + 1 on the next with Shift List. On every second level, shift the division by half a spacing so the diagonals cross between floors.

    Watch out: degrees and radians

    Grasshopper's angle inputs work in radians unless you tell them otherwise. A twist of 90 typed as radians is about 14 full turns. If your tower looks like a spring, check the angle input first.

    Practice

    A 40-floor tower twists 120° in total with a Linear graph. How much does each floor rotate relative to the one below? 3°4°0.3° A Linear graph shares the twist evenly: 120 / 40 = 3° per floor. With a Sigmoid graph instead, where do the floors rotate fastest? At the baseAround mid-heightAt the top A Sigmoid is flat at both ends and steep in the middle, so most of the twist happens around mid-height. The tower should twist 90 degrees from base to top, but the plates spin round many times. Click the component whose input receives the wrong unit. What unit does an angle input expect unless you change it? Rotate's A input expects radians and receives up to 90, which is about 14 turns. Put Radians after Multiplication, or right-click A and choose Degrees.

    Draw a column through the corner of every floor plate of a twisting tower, and find the largest angle a column segment makes with the vertical.
  • Four corner polylines, one per corner.
  • A panel shows the largest lean in degrees, and it grows when you increase the twist.
    •  Explode each plate to get its corners: Discontinuity (Curve, Analysis) or Control Points gives them. Then Flip Matrix so each branch is one corner up the tower. Vector 2Pt between consecutive corners, then Angle (Vector, Vector) against Unit Z. Degrees from Radians' partner, Degrees (Maths, Trigonometry).
    1. Discontinuity on each plate rectangle gives its 4 corners, in a tree by floor. Flip Matrix makes one branch per corner.
    2. Polyline per branch for the columns.
    3. Vector 2Pt from each corner to the same corner one floor up (Shift List, Wrap False), Angle against Unit Z, Degrees, then Sort List and List Item i = −1 for the largest.

    {{sn}}Panels on a curved roof

    By the end you can measure how far each panel of a curved roof is from flat, and choose a geometry that gives flat panels.

    Idea

    Glass and metal panels are cheapest when they are flat. On most curved surfaces, the four corners of a panel do not lie in one plane. The panel is warped, and the amount is called its deviation from flat. Plane Fit (Vector, Plane) finds the plane closest to a set of points and reports how far they are from it.

    Three ways out, each with a cost:

    • Triangles are always flat, but you get twice the panels and more joints.
    • Smaller panels warp less, but there are more of them.
    • A translation surface, made by sliding one curve along another, gives flat four-sided panels on a doubly curved roof. Engineers such as Jörg Schlaich and Hans Schober used this for glass grid shells.

    Try it here

    In Rhino

    1. A hypar from four corner points: 4Point Surface (Surface, Freeform) with two high and two low corners.
    2. Divide Domain² and Isotrim for the panels.
    3. Deconstruct Brep (Surface, Analysis): its V output gives the corner points of each panel, one branch per panel.
    4. Plane Fit on those points. Its dx output is the deviation. Multiply by 1000 for millimetres.
    5. Compare with a tolerance using Larger Than (Maths, Operators), and colour the panels with Custom Preview.

    Practice

    Which panel shape is always flat, whatever the surface? A triangleA squareA hexagon Any three points define a plane, so a triangle can never be warped. On a hypar, you halve the panel size in both directions. Roughly what happens to each panel's deviation? It halvesIt drops to about a quarterIt stays the same On a hypar the warp of a panel grows with its width times its length, so halving both gives about a quarter. You expected one deviation per panel, but the panel shows a single number. Click the component to remove. How many branches reach Plane Fit? Flatten Tree pours the corners of every panel into one list, so Plane Fit fits a single plane to the whole roof. Remove it and Plane Fit runs once per panel branch.

    Build a dome-like roof from two arcs as a translation surface, panel it with quads, and show that every panel is flat.
  • Every panel's deviation is below 0.1 mm.
  • The roof is curved in both directions.
    •  Sum Surface (Surface, Freeform) makes a surface from two edge curves by sliding one along the other.
    1. Two arcs that share an end point: one in the XZ plane, one in the YZ plane.
    2. Sum Surface from the two arcs.
    3. Divide Domain², Isotrim, Deconstruct Brep, Plane Fit as in the session. All dx values are close to zero.
