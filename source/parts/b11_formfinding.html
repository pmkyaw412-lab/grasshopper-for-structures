Form-finding

Some shapes are not designed so much as found. A hanging chain takes the shape that carries its load in pure tension; turned upside down, that shape carries the same load in pure compression. These two sessions use Kangaroo, the physics solver that comes with Grasshopper in Rhino 8, to find such shapes and then check them as structures.

{{sn}}From a hanging chain to an arch

By the end you can form-find an arch with Kangaroo and show, with Karamba, why it carries its load almost without bending.

Idea

Robert Hooke published the idea as a Latin anagram in 1676: as hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch. A chain can only pull, so the shape it hangs in is the line of its forces. Flip it, and every link pushes instead of pulls, still along the line. Gaudí designed the church at Colònia Güell this way, with weighted strings.

  • Under its own weight, spread evenly along its length, a chain hangs as a catenary.
  • Under a load spread evenly along the horizontal, like a deck hung from it, it hangs as a parabola. That is why the parabolic arch in Session {{n:s13}} suits a bridge.

Kangaroo works with goals. Length (Line) holds a link at a length, Anchor holds a point still, Load pushes a point. The Solver moves all the points until every goal is balanced as well as it can be.

Try it here

In Rhino

  1. A 20 m line, divided into links: Divide Curve, then Polyline and Explode, gives the link lines.
  2. On the Kangaroo2 tab, Length (Line) with the links and a length of 1.12 × the link length, so the chain is 12% longer than the span.
  3. End Points of the original line into two Anchor goals.
  4. Load on every point with the vector (0, 0, −1).
  5. Entwine or Merge all the goals into the Solver's GoalObjects input. Its output points form the hanging chain.
  6. Flip: Scale NU (Transform, Affine) with a Z factor of −1. Then Line to Beam in Karamba, pinned supports at both ends, and the same loads pointing down.

Watch out: one load case only

The found shape is free of bending only for the load that found it. A live load on half the span, or wind, bends it. A real arch still needs bending stiffness; form-finding gives you the best starting shape, not a finished design.

    {{sn}}From a hanging net to a gridshell

    By the end you can form-find a gridshell roof, check it in Karamba, and say how its supports change its shape.

    Idea

    The same idea works in 3D. Hang a net from its corners or edges and it takes a shape in pure tension; flip it and you have a gridshell (グリッドシェル) in compression. The Mannheim Multihalle, a timber gridshell by Carlfried Mutschler and Frei Otto with Ove Arup & Partners as engineers, completed in 1975, took its form from a hanging model.

    Which points you anchor changes everything. A net held at its corners sags along its free edges; held on all four edges it forms a dome. Link lengths matter too: equal-length laths are easy to build, so a good gridshell keeps its member lengths close to each other.

    Try it here

    In Rhino

    1. A flat grid: Mesh Plane (Mesh, Primitive), 20 by 20 m with 10 by 10 faces.
    2. Mesh Edges (Mesh, Analysis) gives the links. Length (Line) with 1.1 × their length.
    3. Anchors: the end points of the boundary edges, which Mesh Edges returns from its first output, or the four corner vertices only.
    4. Load on every vertex with (0, 0, −1). Solver, then Scale NU with Z = −1 to flip.
    5. In Karamba: Line to Beam on the flipped edges, pinned supports at the anchored points, and the same loads pointing down. Check that the members are in compression.

    Practice

    2. Fitness = Absolute(rise − 4); Galapagos Minimize; genome = the length factor slider.
